Addendum 51 — Radiative Identity Replacement Occurs Before the Gravitational-Wave Background

Abstract

Addendum 50 identified radiation as a boundary-relative count status.

A subordinate gravitational source relation becomes radiative when changing relational organization is recursively reclassified at an enclosing boundary as a readable occurrence,

\[\chi_R\]

Radiation is therefore not primitive source energy escaping into space.

It is the physical wave read of an enclosing recurrent occurrence.

Recursive replacement does not stop there.

A radiative boundary can distinguish individual gravitational occurrences by labels such as

source, ϕ, n, p, f,

representing source identity, phase, propagation direction, polarization, and recurrence class.

At a still more enclosing boundary, some of those labels may cease to define distinct macrostates.

The realization remains.

Its count meaning changes.

Let

\[B_R\]

denote a gravitational radiative boundary and

\[B_B\]

a more enclosing background boundary.

Define

\[\pi_{B\mid R}:\mathcal M_R\to\mathcal M_B.\]

Two radiative occurrences are background-equivalent when

\[\pi_{B\mid R}(\chi_R^{(\alpha)})=\pi_{B\mid R}(\chi_R^{(\gamma)}).\]

The distinction separating them at BR is then no longer owned by BB .

If several radiative occurrence classes map to one enclosing background class β , their realized occurrence counts combine:

\[b_{B,\beta}=\sum_{\alpha\in\pi_{B\mid R}^{-1}(\beta)}N_\alpha.\]

The logarithmic background coordinate is

\[\Phi_{B,\beta}=\ln b_{B,\beta}.\]

Thus background formation is not count destruction.

It is not necessarily dissipation.

It is not necessarily thermalization.

It is not necessarily loss of recurrence.

It is not defined by low amplitude, large source number, long propagation distance, or cosmological redshift.

It is a many-to-one change in the count domain:

distinguishable radiative occurrences → background degeneracy.

Crucially, background formation does not require every radiative distinction to disappear.

A map such as

\[(s,\phi,\mathbf n,p,f)\longmapsto f\]

can replace individual source, phase, direction, and polarization identity while retaining recurrence class.

The resulting background is therefore spectrally resolved.

Likewise,

\[(s,\phi,\mathbf n,p,f)\longmapsto(N,f)\]

can produce an anisotropic frequency-resolved background.

Thus:

background \(\ne\) featureless.

The same distinction is already visible in blackbody radiation.

Individual electromagnetic emission events need not remain readable at a thermal boundary, while frequency class and occupancy remain measurable.

Blackbody radiation therefore demonstrates that

occurrence identity can disappear while recurrence survives.

The gravitational-wave background realizes the same recursive architecture.

The complete gravitational sequence now contains two successive reclassifications:

\[\mathcal M_S\xrightarrow{\pi_{R\mid S}}\mathcal M_R\xrightarrow{\pi_{B\mid R}}\mathcal M_B\]

In words,

source organization → radiative occurrence → background realization.

The first bridge determines which source distinctions survive as radiation.

The second determines which radiative distinctions survive as structure within background.

Conventional stochastic gravitational-wave observables such as

\[S_h(f),\quad h_c(f),\quad\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}(f)\]

come later.

They are statistical, strain, and energetic projections of a background already defined by recursive count replacement.

They are not the primitive background itself.

Nor does the word stochastic imply primitive randomness.

Statistical description becomes natural after individual occurrence labels have been replaced.

This addendum therefore defines the gravitational-wave background by the loss of individual radiative identity relative to an enclosing count boundary, not by the destruction of radiation.

The central statement is:

a gravitational-wave background begins not when gravitational radiation disappears, weakens, therm

Therefore:

radiative identity replacement occurs before the gravitational-wave background.

1. Begin With the Radiative Occurrence

Addendum 50 established a gravitational radiative occurrence

\[\chi_R\]

at a boundary

\[B_R\]

The occurrence remains gravitationally readable through one or more physical labels.

These may include:

\[(s, \phi, n, p, f , \ldots)\]

The labels describe physical distinctions owned by the radiative boundary.

2. Radiation and Background Are Different Count Statuses

At BR ,

\[\chi_R\]

remains a distinguishable occurrence.

Therefore,

\[\chi_R \ne \Phi_R\]

Radiation can exist in the presence of background without itself already being background.

The distinction is defined by what the boundary continues to count separately.

3. Recursive Replacement Continues Beyond Radiation

The radiative boundary need not be the largest relevant count domain.

Introduce a more enclosing boundary

\[B_B\]

This boundary can organize radiative realizations according to a different macrostate classification.

Some labels readable at BR may no longer distinguish separate states at BB .

4. Define the Background Classification Map

Let

\[\mathcal M_R\]

be the radiative occurrence domain.

Let

\[\mathcal M_B\]

be the enclosing background domain.

Define

\[\pi_{B\mid R}:\mathcal M_R\to\mathcal M_B.\]

This is the second recursive classification map in the gravitational-radiation hierarchy.

5. Define Background Equivalence

For two radiative occurrences,

\[\chi_R^{(\alpha)}\]

and

\[\chi_R^{(\gamma)}\]

define

\[\chi_R^{(\alpha)}\sim_B\chi_R^{(\gamma)}\]

when

\[\pi_{B\mid R}(\chi_R^{(\alpha)})=\pi_{B\mid R}(\chi_R^{(\gamma)}).\]

The distinction separating the two occurrences remains meaningful at BR but no longer defines two macrostates at BB .

6. Background Formation Is Many-to-One Reclassification

Suppose several radiative classes map to a single background class

\[β\]

Then:

\[\{\chi_R^{(\alpha)}\}_{\alpha\in\pi_{B\mid R}^{-1}(\beta)}\longrightarrow\beta\]

The transformation is many-to-one.

This is recursive replacement.

The earlier gravitational-background treatment already identified the central structure as distinguishable radiative occurrences becoming background degeneracy without loss of realized count.

7. Recursive Replacement Is Not Mere Ignorance

It is tempting to say that the waves remain individually defined and the observer simply lacks sufficient information.

That is not the stronger R²D statement.

At boundary BB , the replaced distinctions are not part of the macrostate classification owned by that boundary.

Thus:

The comparison between replacement and hidden readable detail is clipped [page 1756; author review].

The count domain itself has changed.

8. Realized Count Survives Identity Replacement

Suppose radiative class α has realized occurrence count

\[N_\alpha\]

over a specified common comparison domain.

If several classes map to one background class β , define

\[b_{B,\beta}=\sum_{\alpha\in\pi_{B\mid R}^{-1}(\beta)}N_\alpha.\]

The radiative identities disappear from the enclosing classification.

Their realized count does not.

Thus:

identity replacement \(\ne\) count destruction.

9. Background Is Realized Count

Define the logarithmic background coordinate

\[\Phi_{B,\beta}=\ln b_{B,\beta}.\]

Background is therefore already realized count.

It is categorically different from unrealized possibility.

Thus:

\[\Phi\ne P\]

10. Background Is Also Not Native Multiplicity

Native multiplicity is

\[W\]

It describes possible realizations within a macrostate landscape.

Background count is

\[b\]

It describes realized count after relevant subordinate occurrence identities have been replaced.

Therefore,

\[W \ne b\]

and hence

\[S = \ln W \ne \Phi = \ln b\]

A gravitational-wave background is not simply a large multiplicity.

11. Count Before Logarithm

The background count must be assembled before taking its logarithm.

For one background class,

\[b_{B,\beta}=\sum_{\alpha\in\pi_{B\mid R}^{-1}(\beta)}N_\alpha.\]

Only then define

\[\Phi_{B,\beta}=\ln b_{B,\beta}.\]

Therefore,

\[\ln\!\left(\sum_\alpha N_\alpha\right)\ne\sum_\alpha\ln N_\alpha\]

in general.

The counting operation is primitive to the logarithmic coordinate.

12. Radiative Occurrences Carry Multiple Labels

A gravitational radiative occurrence may be schematically represented as

\[\chi_R = \chi_R (s, \phi, n, p, f , \ldots)\]

Here:

\[s\]

labels source or event identity;

\[\phi\]

labels phase;

\[n\]

labels propagation direction;

\[p\]

labels polarization;

\[f\]

labels recurrence class.

The enclosing boundary need not retain all of them.

13. Background Formation Need Not Be All-or-None

Consider

\[\pi_{B\mid R}:(s,\phi,\hat n,p,f)\longrightarrow f\]

The enclosing boundary no longer distinguishes:

\[s, \phi, n, p\]

but still distinguishes

\[f\]

The result is a background that remains spectrally resolved.

Thus:

background \(\not\Rightarrow\) loss of every distinction.

14. Source Identity Can Be Replaced First

Suppose many compact-binary occurrences remain individually distinguishable at BR :

\[\chi_R^{(1)},\quad\chi_R^{(2)},\quad\chi_R^{(3)},\ldots\]

At BB , source identities may cease to define separate states.

Then

\[\chi_R^{(1)}\sim_B\chi_R^{(2)}\sim_B\chi_R^{(3)}\sim_B\cdots\]

within a surviving background class.

The source labels disappear.

The realized count remains.

15. Absolute Phase Can Be Replaced While Frequency Survives

Consider two occurrences of the same recurrence class:

\[(f , \phi1 )\]

and

\[(f , \phi2 )\]

If BB no longer distinguishes absolute phase,

\[(f , \phi1 ) \sim_B (f , \phi2 )\]

Yet

\[f\]

can remain readable.

Therefore:

phase loss \(\not\Rightarrow\) frequency loss.

The old analysis identified precisely this possibility.

16. Directional Identity Can Be Replaced Without Producing Perfect Isotropy

Suppose individual source directions are no longer separable.

This does not require every angular distinction to vanish.

The enclosing boundary may retain coarse angular classes,

\[N\]

Then

\[(s,\phi,\mathbf n,p,f)\longmapsto(N,f)\].

The background remains anisotropic at the larger scale.

17. Background Nondirectionality Is Boundary Relative

Background is nondirectional relative to the subordinate directional classification that has been replaced.

It need not be directionless under every enclosing coordinate.

Thus:

background \(\ne\) perfect isotropy.

An anisotropic gravitational-wave background is completely compatible with the R²D definition.

The earlier treatment already emphasized that background can preserve angular anisotropy and other enclosing structure.

18. Polarization Can Likewise Survive Statistically

Individual occurrence polarization may cease to remain separately readable.

Yet an enclosing polarization imbalance or correlation can remain.

Thus:

The polarization-identity comparison is clipped [page 1761; author review].

The larger boundary owns the surviving statistical distinction.

19. Background Is Therefore Not Featureless

A gravitational-wave background may retain:

• frequency structure; • angular anisotropy; • polarization statistics; • temporal correlations; • spatial correlations; • and other enclosing macrostates.

Thus:

background \(\ne\) featureless count.

Only the replaced subordinate distinctions have disappeared from the local classification.

20. Blackbody Radiation Supplies the Clearest Calibration

This structure is already physically familiar in blackbody radiation.

A thermal boundary does not need to retain the identity and phase of each microscopic emission event.

Yet frequency class remains readable.

The radiation field can therefore be represented by occupancy across recurrence classes.

This gives the physical pattern:

individual occurrence identity lost

while

recurrence class remains.

Blackbody radiation therefore provides a direct calibration for frequency-resolved background realization.

21. A Frequency-Resolved Background Is Still Background

If frequency survives the enclosing classification, define

\[b_B (f )\]

as the realized background count associated with recurrence class f .

Then define

\[\Phi_B (f ) = \ln b_B (f )\]

This does not identify frequency with background.

Rather,

f = surviving recurrence label,

while

ΦB (f ) = logarithmic realized background count in that class.

22. Background Count Is Not Background Recurrence

The distinction is therefore:

\[\Phi_B \ne f_B\]

One is a logarithmic count coordinate.

The other is a physical recurrence read.

The earlier gravitational-background analysis already treated these as categorically different quantities.

23. A Spectrum Is Not the Primitive Background

A frequency-resolved gravitational-wave background can possess a spectrum.

But that spectrum is a physical projection of background occupancy and recurrence.

Therefore:

\[\Phi_B (f ) \ne S_h (f )\]

\[\Phi_B (f ) \ne h_c (f )\]

and

\[\Phi_B (f ) \ne \Omega_{\mathrm{GW}} (f )\]

The background count comes first.

24. Conventional Strain Spectral Density Comes Later

A stochastic gravitational-wave background is conventionally described using a strain power spectral density

\[S_h (f )\]

This quantity describes measured strain variance per physical frequency interval.

R²D therefore places it downstream:

\[\Phi_B \to f \to S_h (f )\]

The detector spectrum does not define the primitive background count.

25. Characteristic Strain Is Also a Downstream Read

Likewise,

\[h_c (f )\]

is a useful physical representation of background strain strength.

The sequence is

background count → recurrence class → strain projection.

Thus:

\[h_c (f ) \ne \Phi_B\]

26. ΩGW (f ) Is an Energetic Projection

Conventional gravitational-wave cosmology defines

\[\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}(f)=\frac1{\rho_c}\frac{d\rho_{\mathrm{GW}}}{d\ln f}\]

This describes gravitational-wave energy density per logarithmic physical frequency interval relative to the critical density.

It is therefore an energetic read.

Thus:

ΦB → f → energetic calibration → ΩGW (f ).

And:

\[\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}} (f ) \ne \Phi_B\]

27. Blackbody and Gravitational Backgrounds Now Share a Formal Pattern

For blackbody radiation:

background count + surviving recurrence class → thermal spectral projection.

For a gravitational-wave background:

background count + surviving recurrence class → strain/statistical/energetic projection.

The physical spectra differ.

The recursive count architecture is the same.

28. Background Need Not Be Thermal

Nothing in

\[\Phi = \ln b\]

requires temperature.

Therefore:

\[\Phi\ne T\]

A gravitational-wave background can be strongly non-Planckian and still be background in the R²D sense.

The previous analysis made this distinction explicit.

29. A Thermal Bath Is One Physical Realization of Background

A thermodynamic bath is a physically important background realization.

Its background can acquire a thermal read through quantities such as

\[T\]

Thus schematically,

Φ → thermal realization → T .

But temperature is not the primitive background.

30. A Stochastic Gravitational Background Is Another Realization

Similarly,

Φ → gravitational radiative background → Sh (f ), hc (f ), ΩGW (f ).

The physical observables are different.

The underlying R²D category remains realized background count.

31. “Bath” Is Therefore Narrower Than Background

A bath is a physical background realization possessing an appropriate thermodynamic read.

Background is the more general count category.

Thus:

bath ⊂ physical realizations of background,

rather than

background = thermal bath

as a universal primitive identity.

32. Stochasticity Comes After Background Formation

The word stochastic can obscure the causal order.

A gravitational-wave background is not primitive because it is random.

Rather, once individual radiative occurrence identities have been replaced, normalized statistical description becomes useful.

Thus:

background realization → statistical description.

Not:

primitive randomness → background.

The older analysis made the same point: many realized radiative distinctions map to fewer enclosing background distinctions, after which statistical description becomes natural.

33. Probability Does Not Create Background Count

Probability distributions may be constructed over background classes.

But the realized counts must exist before normalization.

Thus:

\[b_\beta\longrightarrow p_\beta\]

not

\[p_\beta\longrightarrow b_\beta\]

Probability is a normalized later description.

34. Background Formation Does Not Require Dissipation

A radiative occurrence can be coherently realized and later become background relative to an enclosing boundary simply because its identifying labels are replaced.

No irreversible local thermalization is required.

Thus:

\[\chi_R \to \Phi_B\]

does not imply

radiative dissipation.

This was already an explicit safeguard in the earlier treatment.

35. Recursive Background Formation and Second-Law Background Production Are Different

R²D now contains at least two conceptually different routes into background.

Recursive identity replacement

A formerly distinguishable occurrence becomes degenerate at an enclosing boundary:

\[\chi_R\overset\kappa{\longrightarrow}\Phi_B\]

Irreversible background increment

Occurrence-count nonreciprocity around closure gives

\[\frac{S_{\mathrm{gen}}}{k_B}=\ln\frac{\nu_+}{\nu_-}\]

This defines a Second-Law background increment.

These are not the same operation.

36. The Two Background Routes Can Coexist Physically

A gravitational radiative occurrence can lose source identity under recursive replacement.

It can also later undergo irreversible absorption or thermalization.

Therefore the two processes can occur in sequence or together.

But their logical origins remain distinct:

The comparison between classification loss and occurrence-count nonreciprocity is clipped [page 1769; author review].

37. Background Formation Is Not Energy Destruction

If a radiative occurrence becomes background at BB , its previous identifying distinction is replaced.

That does not imply that its energetic physical read has vanished.

Thus:

identity loss \(\ne\) energy destruction.

The physical energy may remain encoded in downstream background observables.

38. Background Formation Is Not Amplitude Loss

A gravitational wave may satisfy approximately

\[h\propto\frac1r\]

in an appropriate propagation regime.

But attenuation of amplitude does not itself change the count classification.

A weak wave can remain individually distinguishable.

Therefore:

The amplitude/background comparison is clipped [page 1769; author review].

39. A Strong Signal Can Still Belong to Background

Conversely, large amplitude does not guarantee individual occurrence identity.

Several strong unresolved occurrence classes may map into one enclosing background class.

Thus:

signal strength

is not the primitive background criterion.

40. Source Number Is Not the Primitive Criterion

A very large number of sources may remain individually resolved.

Conversely, a single occurrence may fail to retain its source identity at a particular boundary.

Therefore,

Nsource ≫ 1

is neither necessary nor sufficient for background.

The essential condition is the enclosing many-to-one classification.

41. Cosmological Redshift Is Also a Different Operation

A propagating gravitational occurrence may undergo

\[f_{\mathrm{emit}} \to f_{\mathrm{obs}}\]

through cosmological expansion.

This changes its physical recurrence calibration.

It does not necessarily change its occurrence identity.

Therefore:

redshift \(\ne\) recursive replacement.

The prior treatment likewise distinguished redshift from background formation.

42. A Redshifted Wave Can Remain Individually Resolved

If source identity, phase evolution, sky direction, or another occurrence label remains readable after redshift, then the signal remains a radiative occurrence:

\[\chi_R\]

It has changed frequency.

It has not necessarily become background.

43. Resolved Radiation and Background Can Coexist

One observational domain may contain both:

\[\chi_R^{\mathrm{resolved}}\]

and

\[\Phi_B^{\mathrm{unresolved}}\]

Some occurrence classes remain individually readable.

Others have undergone background classification.

There is no contradiction.

Different classes can cross the readability boundary under different conditions.

44. Background Is Boundary Relative Without Being Arbitrary

Whether occurrence identity remains readable can depend on:

• detector sensitivity; • observation interval; • angular resolution; • recurrence bandwidth; • source density; • signal overlap; • propagation history; • and comparison domain.

These are physical conditions defining the receiving boundary.

Thus:

boundary relative \(\ne\) arbitrary.

Once the boundary is specified, the classification is objective relative to it.

45. Observation Time Belongs to the Count Boundary

A signal unresolved over a short comparison interval may become distinguishable over a longer one.

Thus the relevant boundary includes not only spatial or instrumental resolution but the recurrence comparison domain.

A longer observation can change which states remain distinguishable.

This is fully consistent with boundary-indexed recurrence counting.

46. Improved Measurement Does Not Reverse Recursive Ontology

Suppose a source previously counted as part of a stochastic background becomes individually resolvable.

This does not mean that reality physically evolved backward from background into radiation.

The physical readout boundary has changed.

The new boundary now owns a finer distinction.

Thus:

finer readability \(\ne\) reverse physical carry.

47. The Same Realization Can Have Different Count Meanings

At one boundary, the realization can be counted as

\[\Phi_B\]

At another,

\[\chi'_B\]
